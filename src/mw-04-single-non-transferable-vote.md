# Single Non-Transferable Vote (SNTV)

## One Vote, Multiple Seats

---

## Statement of Purpose

The previous articles examined three ways to modify bloc voting's ballot constraint. Limited voting reduces the number of votes. Cumulative voting preserves the number but allows concentration. Each modification creates structural space for minority representation -- but each also depends on strategic coordination.

The Single Non-Transferable Vote takes the simplification to its logical conclusion. Each voter receives exactly one vote -- the same ballot as single-winner plurality -- but the election fills multiple seats. The ballot could not be simpler.

That simplicity comes at a cost. SNTV introduces the most intense coordination demands of any method examined in this Part. Under cumulative voting, voters can self-coordinate by concentrating their votes. Under SNTV, that option does not exist. Different voters must go to different candidates -- a challenge that falls on parties and political organizations rather than individual voters. SNTV also introduces a dynamic not seen in the previous methods: direct competition between candidates of the same party.

This article examines how SNTV works, where it is used, and what its strategic dynamics reveal about the relationship between ballot simplicity and coordination complexity.

---

## Currently Used

SNTV is used extensively in Connecticut, where state laws create one-vote multi-seat elections for numerous local boards and commissions. Puerto Rico uses SNTV for its territorial legislature. In Alabama, multiple small towns adopted SNTV as part of Voting Rights Act remedies under the *Dillard v. Town of Cuba* settlements. Euclid, Ohio uses a functionally identical system -- one vote for three school board seats -- under a 2009 court order.

SNTV is the limiting case of limited voting: limited voting where voters have exactly one vote. The distinction between "limited voting with one vote" and "SNTV" is definitional rather than mechanical. The system works the same way. The separate name reflects SNTV's distinct strategic properties and its independent history in political science.

---

## Section 1: How SNTV Works

In an SNTV election with K seats:

1. Each voter casts exactly one vote for one candidate.
2. All votes are tallied.
3. The K candidates with the highest vote totals are elected.

The ballot is identical to single-winner plurality. The counting rule is identical to single-winner plurality. The only difference is that K candidates win instead of one.

### The Simplest Possible Multi-Winner Ballot

SNTV uses the simplest ballot available in multi-winner elections. A voter sees a list of candidates and marks one. No rankings, no scores, no multiple selections. Any voter who has cast a single-winner plurality ballot already knows how to participate in an SNTV election.

This simplicity is genuine -- but it shifts complexity from the ballot to the strategic environment. Under cumulative voting, the voter decides how to allocate multiple votes. Under SNTV, each voter's decision is binary: which one candidate to support. The strategic question of how a group's votes are distributed across candidates becomes someone else's problem.

## Section 2: A Worked Example

Return to the city council election: three seats, 100 voters, and the same two groups. Group A (60 voters) supports Adams, Allen, and Ayers. Group B (40 voters) supports Brooks, Bailey, and Burns.

Each voter casts one vote.

### Scenario A: Efficient Coordination by Both Groups

Group A nominates two candidates and splits its 60 votes evenly: Adams receives 30, Allen receives 30. Group B nominates one candidate: Brooks receives all 40 votes.

| Candidate | Votes |
|---|---|
| Brooks | 40 |
| Adams | 30 |
| Allen | 30 |

Three candidates, three seats. Group A wins two seats, Group B wins one. The outcome is roughly proportional to each group's share of the electorate.

### Scenario B: Group A Over-Nominates

Group A runs three candidates and splits its 60 votes evenly: Adams 20, Allen 20, Ayers 20. Group B runs one candidate: Brooks 40.

| Candidate | Votes |
|---|---|
| Brooks | 40 |
| Adams | 20 |
| Allen | 20 |
| Ayers | 20 |

Group B wins one seat; Group A wins two (with the third seat going to one of its candidates at 20 votes). The outcome is still proportional -- but only because Group B ran the right number of candidates. If Group B had also run three candidates and its 40 votes had fragmented, the results could have shifted.

### Scenario C: Vote Management Failure

Group A runs three candidates but its votes cluster unevenly: Adams 35, Allen 20, Ayers 5. Group B runs two candidates: Brooks 25, Bailey 15.

| Candidate | Votes |
|---|---|
| Adams | 35 |
| Brooks | 25 |
| Allen | 20 |
| Bailey | 15 |
| Ayers | 5 |

The top three are Adams (35), Brooks (25), and Allen (20). Group A wins two seats, Group B wins one. The outcome happens to be proportional, but 5 of Group A's votes are effectively wasted on Ayers, and 15 of Group B's votes are wasted on Bailey. A slight shift -- a few more voters choosing Ayers instead of Adams, or Bailey instead of Brooks -- could have changed which individuals won.

### What the Scenarios Reveal

Under SNTV, the proportionality of the outcome depends almost entirely on how votes are distributed across candidates within each group. The system does not adjust for uneven distribution. Votes that pile up on a candidate who would have won anyway are surplus -- they help no one else. Votes that go to a candidate who finishes too far behind are wasted. The distance between a proportional outcome and a distorted one can be a handful of voters making different choices.

## Section 3: Intra-Party Competition

SNTV introduces a dynamic that does not exist in the other methods examined so far: **intra-party competition**.

Under bloc voting, limited voting, and cumulative voting, candidates from the same party or faction are not in direct competition with each other for individual votes. Under bloc voting and limited voting, a voter can support multiple candidates from the same group. Under cumulative voting, a voter can concentrate on one but is distributing a pool of votes that could go to several.

Under SNTV, each voter marks one candidate. When multiple candidates from the same party are running, they compete directly against each other for those individual votes. A vote for Adams is a vote not cast for Allen or Ayers -- even though all three represent Group A.

This creates a structural tension between the party's collective interest and each candidate's individual interest. The party wants its candidates to divide the vote evenly. Perfect vote distribution maximizes the party's seat count: if Group A has 60 votes and runs two candidates, the ideal split is 30-30 rather than 45-15 (which wastes 15 votes as surplus on the leading candidate).

But individual candidates have an incentive to attract as many personal votes as possible, even at the expense of their co-partisans. A candidate who draws votes away from a running mate may secure their own seat while costing the party another. The candidate's rational self-interest and the party's collective interest can pull in opposite directions.

## Section 4: The Coordination Problem

SNTV reveals the coordination problem that runs through all the methods examined in this Part, but it does so in the starkest possible form.

### The Party's Dilemma

The party must make two decisions before the election:

**How many candidates to nominate.** Too many candidates splits the vote and risks electing fewer than the group's share. Too few candidates wastes votes as surplus -- support that could have elected an additional seat goes unused. The optimal number depends on the group's expected vote share and the number of seats, but getting it right requires accurate estimates of both.

**How to distribute supporters across candidates.** Even if the party nominates the right number of candidates, the votes must be distributed roughly evenly among them. Under cumulative voting, individual voters can self-coordinate -- a voter who wants to help the party's slate can plump for the candidate who needs it most. Under SNTV, that option does not exist. Each voter can support only one candidate. Distributing voters across candidates requires party-level coordination: geographic allocation, endorsement strategies, messaging campaigns, or other mechanisms that steer different voters to different candidates.

### Why This Is Harder Than It Sounds

The challenge is that the party cannot control where individual votes go. It can suggest, endorse, and strategize, but voters make their own choices. If a popular candidate draws more support than planned, the surplus is wasted. If an unpopular candidate draws less, their votes may fall below the threshold needed to win a seat. The party is trying to solve a distribution problem using tools that cannot guarantee a specific distribution.

This party-level coordination challenge is what political scientists call **vote management** in the SNTV context. The term was introduced in the limited voting article as a general concept. Under SNTV, vote management reaches its highest stakes: the ballot provides no mechanism for voters to express anything beyond a single choice, so the entire burden of achieving proportional distribution falls on organizational coordination.

## Section 5: Structural Properties

### Where SNTV Sits on the Spectrum

SNTV is the fourth method examined in this Part, and it occupies a distinctive position. The ballot is the simplest of any multi-winner method -- identical to single-winner plurality. The coordination demands are the highest.

This inverse relationship between ballot simplicity and coordination complexity is not a coincidence. As the ballot provides less information about each voter's preferences, the system has less to work with. The counting rule cannot adjust for strategic mistakes because it knows nothing about the voter's broader preferences -- only which single candidate they chose. The work of producing a proportional outcome falls entirely on the strategic environment outside the ballot.

### What SNTV Shares with the Previous Methods

Like bloc voting, limited voting, and cumulative voting, SNTV does not contain any mechanism that adjusts seat allocation to reflect voter support. There is no quota, no reweighting, and no transfer of votes. The counting rule is the same: highest totals win. SNTV is semi-proportional: proportional outcomes are achievable but contingent on strategic coordination.

### What SNTV Introduces

SNTV introduces two concepts not present in the previous methods:

**Intra-party competition.** Candidates from the same faction compete directly for individual votes. This tension between individual and collective interest is a structural consequence of the one-vote ballot.

**Maximum coordination burden.** The party must manage both the number of candidates and the distribution of voters across them, with no ballot mechanism to assist. This is the most demanding form of vote management examined in this Part.

### Candidate Incentives

While we cannot attribute all candidate and campaign behavior to the voting system, we can identify the structural incentives it puts in place.

Under SNTV, candidates face a dual imperative: attract enough personal votes to win a seat, but not so many that a running mate is starved of support. This creates incentives for candidates to cultivate personal followings -- geographic bases, community ties, individual name recognition -- that are distinct from and sometimes in tension with the party's broader strategy.

In systems where SNTV has been used at scale, such as Japan's House of Representatives (which used SNTV from 1947 to 1993), this dynamic contributed to candidate-centered rather than party-centered campaigns, with significant consequences for party organization and legislative behavior. That level of analysis is beyond the scope of this article, but the structural incentive is visible in the mechanics themselves.

### Administrative Properties

SNTV shares the administrative strengths of single-winner plurality: simple ballot design (mark one name), single-round counting, easy auditing, and fast results. No election infrastructure changes are required to move from single-winner plurality to SNTV -- only the number of winners changes.

---

## Conclusion

SNTV is the simplest multi-winner ballot possible: one vote per voter, multiple seats filled. That simplicity masks the most demanding coordination environment of any method examined in this Part. The system creates structural space for minority representation -- but extracting proportional outcomes requires party-level vote management that the ballot itself does nothing to facilitate.

The four methods examined so far -- bloc voting, limited voting, cumulative voting, and SNTV -- all use simple ballots: mark one or more candidates, distribute votes, or choose one. They form a spectrum from fully majoritarian (bloc voting) to semi-proportional (SNTV), with each step modifying how many votes the voter receives or how those votes can be distributed.

The next article examines a method that departs from this family entirely. Multi-Seat STAR uses the score ballot introduced in the previous Part -- voters rate every candidate on a 0-5 scale -- and applies the two-phase STAR process (scoring round plus automatic runoff) seat by seat. The ballot collects fundamentally different information. The question is whether that information changes the structural outcome.
