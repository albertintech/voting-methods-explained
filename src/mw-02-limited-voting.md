# Limited Voting

## The First Modification

---

## Statement of Purpose

The previous article examined bloc voting -- the most common multi-winner election format in American government -- and demonstrated the sweep effect: a cohesive majority can capture every available seat.

Limited voting is the first modification. The counting rule is identical to bloc voting: the K candidates with the most votes win. The only change is the ballot constraint: voters receive fewer votes than there are seats.

This single change has significant consequences. It creates structural space for minority representation that bloc voting does not provide. But it does not guarantee proportional outcomes. Whether minorities achieve representation depends on strategic coordination -- a dependency that makes limited voting **semi-proportional** rather than fully proportional.

This article examines how limited voting works, where it is used, and what its structural properties reveal about the relationship between ballot design and representational outcomes.

---

## Currently Used

Limited voting has deep roots in American elections.

Pennsylvania county commissioners have been elected under a limited voting system since an 1871 constitutional amendment, with voters casting two votes for three seats -- one of the oldest continuous uses in the United States. Philadelphia's City Council uses limited voting at significant urban scale: voters may vote for five candidates to fill seven at-large seats, a structure established under the 1951 Home Rule Charter.

Connecticut state laws enacted in 1949 and 1959 created limited voting systems for most local boards and commissions. As of 2021, at least 42 boards and town councils in Connecticut use limited voting.

Limited voting has also been adopted in more than 20 municipalities across Alabama and North Carolina as Voting Rights Act remedies -- alternatives to the at-large bloc voting systems that VRA litigation challenged.

## Section 1: How Limited Voting Works

In a limited voting election with K seats:

1. Each voter may vote for up to L candidates, where L is less than K. Each vote goes to a different candidate.
2. All votes are tallied.
3. The K candidates with the highest vote totals are elected.

The counting rule is identical to bloc voting. What changes is the ballot: voters have fewer votes than seats. In Pennsylvania's county commissioner elections, voters cast two votes for three seats (L = 2, K = 3). In Philadelphia's City Council, voters cast five votes for seven seats (L = 5, K = 7).

### Why Fewer Votes Matter

Under bloc voting, a majority can cover every seat because it has enough votes to support a full slate. If there are five seats and each voter has five votes, a 60-voter majority can place a vote on each of its five candidates. Every majority candidate receives 60 votes, and every minority candidate receives fewer.

Under limited voting, the majority no longer has enough votes to cover every seat. If there are three seats but each voter has only one vote, a 60-voter majority must choose how to distribute those 60 votes across its candidates. It cannot give 60 votes to all three. The majority must spread thinner, and the minority can concentrate.

This is the structural mechanism that softens the sweep effect. It does not eliminate the sweep -- a majority can still win every seat under some configurations -- but it changes the strategic terrain in ways that create an opening for minority representation.

## Section 2: A Worked Example

Return to the city council election from the previous article: three seats, 100 voters, six candidates. Group A (60 voters) supports Adams, Allen, and Ayers. Group B (40 voters) supports Brooks, Bailey, and Burns.

Under limited voting with one vote per voter (L = 1, K = 3):

### Scenario A: Group B Concentrates, Group A Spreads

Group B directs all 40 votes to Brooks. Group A splits its 60 votes evenly across its three candidates.

| Candidate | Votes |
|---|---|
| Brooks | 40 |
| Adams | 20 |
| Allen | 20 |
| Ayers | 20 |
| Bailey | 0 |
| Burns | 0 |

The top three are Brooks, Adams, and Allen. Group A wins two seats; Group B wins one. The 40 percent minority has achieved representation.

### Scenario B: Group A Adapts

Group A recognizes the risk and concentrates its 60 votes on two candidates instead of three.

| Candidate | Votes |
|---|---|
| Brooks | 40 |
| Adams | 30 |
| Allen | 30 |
| Ayers | 0 |
| Bailey | 0 |
| Burns | 0 |

The top three are Brooks (40), Adams (30), and Allen (30). The result is the same: Group A wins two seats, Group B wins one. Even when Group A adapts its strategy, Group B's concentration on a single candidate guarantees it a seat.

### Scenario C: Coordination Failure

If Group B splits its votes between two candidates instead of concentrating:

| Candidate | Votes |
|---|---|
| Adams | 30 |
| Allen | 30 |
| Brooks | 20 |
| Bailey | 20 |
| Ayers | 0 |
| Burns | 0 |

The top three are Adams (30), Allen (30), and either Brooks or Bailey (20, requiring a tiebreaker). Group B still wins one seat, but the margin is thinner. And if Group B had split its votes three ways -- Brooks 14, Bailey 13, Burns 13 -- Group A could have swept all three seats by concentrating on three candidates at 20 votes each.

### What the Scenarios Reveal

The same electorate, the same preferences, the same method -- but different outcomes depending on how each group distributes its votes. Under bloc voting, Group A's structural advantage determined the result regardless of strategy. Under limited voting, strategy determines whether the minority achieves representation or gets shut out.

This is what it means for a system to create structural space without providing structural guarantees.

## Section 3: Vote Management

The scenarios above illustrate the central strategic challenge of limited voting: **vote management**.

Vote management is the problem of deciding how many candidates to support and how to distribute votes among them. Both groups face it, but the stakes are different.

For the minority, the core tradeoff is between concentration and ambition. Concentrating all votes on one candidate maximizes that candidate's vote total and may guarantee a seat. Running two candidates divides the vote and risks falling below the threshold. Running three divides it further and risks winning nothing. The fewer candidates a group supports, the safer each one's position -- but the fewer seats the group can win.

For the majority, the core tradeoff is between coverage and dilution. Spreading votes across many candidates may leave each one with a lower total than the minority's concentrated candidate. Concentrating on fewer candidates secures those seats but concedes the rest. The majority must decide how many seats it can realistically hold without spreading too thin.

Vote management is not a voter-level decision alone. Individual voters cannot coordinate spontaneously to distribute themselves evenly across candidates. It requires organization -- between parties and voters, or among voters who share political interests. Campaign messaging, endorsement strategies, and slate-building all become tools of vote management.

The effectiveness of limited voting as a tool for minority representation depends heavily on whether minority groups can manage their votes. This is the central limitation of semi-proportional systems: they create the possibility of proportional outcomes, but they place the burden of achieving those outcomes on the voters and parties rather than on the counting rule.

## Section 4: Structural Properties

### What Limited Voting Changes

Limited voting modifies exactly one element of bloc voting: the number of votes per voter. The counting rule, the ballot format (mark individual candidates), and the winner-selection mechanism (top K vote-getters) are all unchanged.

This single modification:

- Softens the sweep effect by preventing the majority from covering every seat at full strength
- Creates structural space for minority representation through vote concentration
- Introduces vote management as a strategic requirement for both groups
- Makes outcomes contingent on coordination, not just preference

### What Limited Voting Does Not Change

Limited voting does not contain any mechanism that adjusts seat allocation to reflect voter support. There is no quota, no reweighting, and no transfer of votes. If a minority group fails to coordinate, it can still be shut out entirely. If a majority group coordinates well, it can still sweep -- the sweep is softened, not eliminated.

Limited voting is **semi-proportional**: proportional outcomes are possible but not guaranteed. The system creates an opening; strategic behavior determines whether anyone walks through it.

### Candidate Incentives

While we cannot attribute all candidate and campaign behavior to the voting system, we can identify the structural incentives it puts in place.

Under limited voting, candidates face a strategic environment that differs from bloc voting in one important respect: the number of viable candidacies is constrained. A group that runs too many candidates risks splitting its limited votes. This creates pressure to limit the field -- to agree on which candidates will run and which will stand aside.

For majority-group candidates, the incentive is to coordinate with co-partisans to avoid dilution. For minority-group candidates, the incentive is to be the consensus choice of a concentrated bloc -- the candidate the group rallies behind. In both cases, intra-group coordination is rewarded and fragmentation is punished.

---

## Conclusion

Limited voting demonstrates that a single structural change -- reducing the number of votes below the number of seats -- can meaningfully alter the representational dynamics of a multi-winner election.

Under bloc voting, a cohesive majority sweeps every seat by design. Under limited voting, the majority can no longer cover every seat at full strength. The minority gains an opening -- but only if it can coordinate effectively. The system is semi-proportional: it creates the possibility of proportional outcomes without guaranteeing them.

This raises a natural question. Limited voting creates space for minority representation by restricting the number of votes. What if, instead of restricting votes, the system allowed voters to concentrate them?

The next article examines cumulative voting -- a method that preserves the number of votes but allows voters to distribute them freely, including giving all of them to a single candidate.
